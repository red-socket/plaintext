<Step n={1} title="A and B, five at a time">

In 1605 Francis Bacon described a cipher made from only two symbols, A and B, in groups of five. AAAAA is A, AAAAB is B, AAABA is C, and so on: it's binary, centuries before computers. HELLO is `AABBB AABAA ABABB ABABB ABBBA`.

<BaconTool client:visible text="AABBB AABAA ABABB ABABB ABBBA" />

<QuickCheck id="bacon-b" answer="B" note="AAAAB is 00001, the second letter.">Decode `AAAAB`.</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Hiding it">

Bacon's real idea wasn't the code, it was where to put it. Any text written in **two styles** can carry it: two typefaces, bold and plain, capitals and lowercase, two colours. The text says one thing; the styles say another.

<QuickCheck id="bacon-case" answer="M" note="hELlo is A B B A A, which is 01100, 12, the thirteenth letter: M.">If capitals mean B and lowercase means A, what letter is hidden in `hELlo`?</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="24 or 26 letters">

Bacon's original alphabet had 24 letters: I and J shared a code, and so did U and V. Modern puzzles usually give every letter its own. If a decode is close but a few letters are off by one, switch versions.

<QuickCheck id="bacon-24" answer="U" note="In the 24-letter version, U and V share a code, and so do I and J.">In Bacon's original 24-letter alphabet, which letter shares a code with V?</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="How to spot it">

- **Two symbols in groups of five**: A/B, or anything else.
- A text where **two styles alternate for no reason**: odd capitals, mixed fonts, some words bold.
- A letter count that's a multiple of five.

If two symbols read as nonsense, swap which one is A.

</Step>
