<Step n={1} title="Two keys">

Diffie–Hellman lets two people agree on a key. **RSA**, published in 1977 by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman, goes further: a **public key** anyone can use to encrypt a message to you, and a **private key** only you can use to decrypt it. (Clifford Cocks at GCHQ had found it in 1973; it was declassified in 1997.)

To make a key, pick two primes p and q, and multiply them: **n = p × q**.

<QuickCheck id="rsa-n" answer={["55", "FIFTY FIVE"]} note="5 × 11 = 55.">With p = 5 and q = 11, what is n?</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Encrypt and decrypt">

Choose a public exponent **e**. The public key is (n, e); to encrypt a number m, compute **c = mᵉ mod n**. The private exponent **d** is chosen so that raising to e and then to d brings every number back: **m = cᵈ mod n**. Working out d needs (p − 1)(q − 1), which needs p and q.

<QuickCheck id="rsa-decrypt" answer={["2", "TWO"]} note="8²⁷ mod 55 = 2: decryption undoes encryption.">The private exponent for that key is 27. Decrypt 8.</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Why it's hard to break">

Everything rests on one fact: multiplying two primes is easy, and splitting the result back into them is not. Factor n and you can work out d. With four-digit n that takes a moment; real keys have more than 600 digits.

Encrypting one letter at a time, as the puzzles here do, is also weak: each letter always gives the same number, so it's a substitution cipher in disguise. Real systems encrypt big blocks with random padding.

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Signatures">

RSA also works backwards. "Encrypt" a number with your **private** key and anyone can check it with your **public** key: if raising the signature to e gives back the number, only the private key's owner could have made it. That's a **digital signature**.

</Step>

<Step n={5} title="How to spot it">

- **Numbers bigger than 26**, often all below some n, with a public key **(n, e)** nearby.
- Words like *public key*, *modulus* or *exponent*.
- A small n: factor it first.

</Step>
