<Step n={1} title="Short, long and gaps">

Morse code writes every letter as a pattern of short signals (**dots**) and long ones (**dashes**). The gaps matter too: a short gap between letters, a longer one between words. On paper, letters are separated by spaces and words by a slash.

The common letters got the short codes. E is a single dot and T a single dash. Rare letters like Q (`--.-`) are long.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Hearing it">

Morse was made to be heard. Operators learn letters as whole sounds, not by counting dots. Press play and listen: a short word, sent slowly.

<Step n={3} title="Morse in disguise">

Anything with two lengths can carry Morse: long and short blinks, taps, tall and short letters, bold and plain words, even the words "long" and "short" themselves. The separators can be disguised too.

When you suspect Morse, find the gaps first. Once you know where each letter ends, which symbol is the dot is easy to try both ways.

<QuickCheck id="morse-disguise-c" answer="C" note="OXOX is -.-. in disguise, which is C.">If X is a dot and O is a dash, what letter is `OXOX`?</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="How to spot it">

- **Dots and dashes**, or any two symbols of different weight, with gaps.
- **Beeps, flashes, blinks or taps** in a sound or video.
- **Groups of one to five symbols** between separators; digits are always five.

Morse is in the [field guide](/field-guide#two-symbols) next to binary and Bacon's cipher, its two-symbol cousins.

</Step>
