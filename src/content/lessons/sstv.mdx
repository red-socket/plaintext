<Step n={1} title="Pictures by radio">

**Slow-scan television** (SSTV) sends a still picture as sound, one line at a time. Copthorne Macdonald developed it in the late 1950s so radio amateurs could swap pictures over ordinary voice channels. A picture takes a minute or two.

It's still in use: the International Space Station sends SSTV pictures during special events, and anyone with a cheap radio can receive them.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Brightness is pitch">

Each pixel becomes a tone between **1500 Hz (black)** and **2300 Hz (white)**. Every line starts with a short **1200 Hz sync pulse**, which is the regular clicking you hear. Before the picture, a header of steady tones says which **mode** follows: how big the picture is and how its colours are sent.

The most common amateur mode, **Martin M1**, sends 256 lines of 320 pixels, each line as green, then blue, then red. It takes just under two minutes.

<Step n={4} title="How to spot it">

- A minute or more of **fast warbling** that starts with **two steady tones**.
- **Regular clicks**, a few per second: one per line.
- Space stations, radio amateurs or old television in the story.

If one mode decodes to noise, the picture is probably in another. This decoder reads Martin M1; dedicated programs read them all.

</Step>
