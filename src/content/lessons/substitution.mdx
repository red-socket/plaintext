<Step n={1} title="Any alphabet will do">

A Caesar shift slides the alphabet along. A **substitution cipher** scrambles it completely: every letter gets a stand-in, chosen however you like. There are about 4 × 10²⁶ ways to scramble 26 letters, so trying every key is hopeless.

The oldest fixed scramble is **Atbash**: the alphabet backwards. A becomes Z, B becomes Y. It comes from Hebrew writing and is still a puzzle favourite, because it undoes itself.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Keyword alphabets">

A random alphabet is hard to remember, so people often build one from a **keyword**: write the keyword, drop repeated letters, then add the rest of the alphabet in order. ZEBRAS gives `ZEBRASCDFGHIJKLMNOPQTUVWXY`. Plain A becomes Z, B becomes E, C becomes B.

<QuickCheck id="subst-zebras-b" answer="E" note="The cipher alphabet starts Z E B R A S, so the second letter, B, becomes E.">With the keyword ZEBRAS, what does plain B become?</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Count the letters">

In the 9th century the scholar al-Kindi noticed the weakness: a substitution changes the letters but not how often they appear. In English, E is the most common letter, then T, A, O, I and N. Count the ciphertext, and its most common letter is probably E.

Letter counts get you started. Word shapes finish the job:

- **One-letter words** are A or I.
- **Common short words**: THE, AND, THAT, WITH. A three-letter word that appears over and over is almost certainly THE.
- **Apostrophes**: a single letter after one is usually S or T.
- **Double letters**: LL, EE, SS, OO, TT are the usual suspects.

<Step n={4} title="A way in">

A good order of work:

1. Count the letters. Mark the top one or two as E and T.
2. Find a repeated three-letter word and try THE.
3. Fill in what you know everywhere, and look at half-finished words. `TH_T` is THAT; `_E_E` with a gap might be HERE or WERE.
4. Expect to be wrong sometimes. Clear a guess and try again.

<QuickCheck id="subst-zit" answer="THE" note="The most common three-letter word in English, by a long way.">A cryptogram contains the three-letter word `ZIT` again and again. What English word is it most likely to be?</QuickCheck>

</Step>

<Step n={5} title="How to spot it">

- Letters only, with normal word shapes, but **no Caesar shift works**.
- Letter counts look like English, **on the wrong letters**.
- Repeated words and double letters survive.
- Atbash in particular: `GSV` for THE, and lots of letters from the end of the alphabet.

</Step>
