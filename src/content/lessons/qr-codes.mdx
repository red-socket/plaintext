<Step n={1} title="Barcodes">

The barcode on almost everything you buy is an **EAN-13**: thirteen digits drawn as 95 thin stripes, black or white. The first digit isn't drawn at all; it's hidden in which of two patterns the next six digits use. The last digit is a check digit, so a misread is caught.

<Step n={2} title="QR codes">

**QR codes** were invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara at Denso Wave, a Japanese car-parts maker, to track parts on assembly lines. The three big squares in the corners tell a scanner where the code is and which way up.

Most of the rest isn't the message. It's **error correction**: extra data that lets the code be read even when part of it is missing. At the highest level, a QR code survives losing about 30 percent of itself. Codes printed white on black still read too.

<Step n={3} title="How to spot it">

- **Three square markers** in the corners of a dotted square: a QR code, even if it's damaged, drawn in pixel art, or inverted.
- **Stripes of different widths** in a row: a barcode, even without numbers printed under it.

Scan first, then look at what comes out: it may be another code.

</Step>
